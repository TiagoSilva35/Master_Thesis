\chapter{Literature Review}
\label{chap:literature-review}
This chapter reviews the work on which a causal, fair and drift-aware recourse method can build.
Section~\ref{sec:lr-cf-search} covers how counterfactuals are searched for, and
Section~\ref{sec:lr-plausibility} how they are kept plausible and causally consistent.
Section~\ref{sec:lr-fair-recourse} asks whether recourse is fair, and Section~\ref{sec:lr-drift}
examines what happens when models, populations or causal mechanisms change over time.
Finally, Section~\ref{sec:lr-readable-structural-equations} turns to readable representations of
structural equations, including those based on Kolmogorov--Arnold Networks (KANs).

\section{Counterfactual Search Methods}
\label{sec:lr-cf-search}
In this section, we analyse the current landscape of counterfactual search methods, focusing on
which strategies are used to find valid counterfactuals and which trade-offs they make between
search efficiency, plausibility and causal consistency. We group the methods into three categories:
exact solver-based methods, gradient-based methods, and evolutionary and surrogate-based methods.

\subsection{Exact Solver-Based Methods}
\label{subsec:lr-solver-search}

\citet{russell2019efficient} proposes a mixed-integer programming approach to generating
counterfactual explanations for linear classifiers, including logistic regression, linear regression
and support vector machines. The search is formulated as an optimisation problem that minimises a
weighted $\ell_1$ distance between the original instance and the counterfactual, which keeps changes
sparse (i.e., as few features as possible are changed). Its main contribution is a set of constraints,
called a mixed polytope, that guarantees coherent counterfactuals: solutions that map back onto the
underlying data structure, even when a variable can take either a continuous value or one of several
discrete states. Diversity is obtained by restricting the variables altered in previously generated
counterfactuals. Results on the LSAT and FICO datasets show that the method finds counterfactuals that
are close to the original instance and cross the model's decision boundary, while avoiding the
brute-force enumeration required by earlier approaches. However, the approach is designed for linear
models, so it does not extend directly to complex neural networks. It is also blind to causal
relationships between features: it guarantees coherence, not causal consistency. Finally, explanations
are presented as short text statements, and the author notes that a complete set can be overwhelming
and that more work is needed on how to present and navigate it interactively.

\citet{kanamori2020dace} also rely on mathematical optimisation, using mixed-integer linear optimisation
for linear models and tree ensembles, but do so in a distribution-aware manner. Their cost function
combines the Mahalanobis distance, which accounts for feature correlations, with the local outlier
factor, which avoids counterfactuals in low-density regions. However, this comes at a computational
price: on the FICO dataset with a random forest, DACE took 519 seconds on average under a 600-second
limit, against 22 to 34 seconds for the baselines. Moreover, although it encourages counterfactuals not
to break correlations, it does not guarantee causal consistency.

\citet{karimi2020model} propose MACE, a model-agnostic method that generates counterfactuals using
Satisfiability Modulo Theories (SMT) solvers. The model, the distance function and the constraints are
all translated into logic formulae, and additional constraints make the counterfactuals plausible and
diverse. The method finds a counterfactual for every individual considered (100\% coverage) at provably
optimal distances. The problem is that the model must be expressible in first-order logic, which may
not be feasible for all types of models, especially complex ones. Once again, the method is blind to
causal relationships between features, and it can be computationally expensive because its running
time depends directly on the solver: under five seconds for logistic regression, but between one
minute and three hours for a multilayer perceptron.

\subsection{Gradient-Based Methods}
\label{subsec:lr-gradient-search}
\citet{vanlooveren2020interpretable} use class prototypes to guide the counterfactual search. A term
added to the loss function encourages the counterfactual to be close to the prototype of the desired
class, obtained either from an encoder or from class-specific k-d trees, which makes the generated
counterfactuals more interpretable and keeps them closer to the data manifold. Evaluation on the MNIST
and Breast Cancer Wisconsin datasets shows that the method generates counterfactuals that are close to
the original instance and cross the decision boundary, and that it is faster than searches without
prototypes. It also removes the bottleneck of numerical gradient evaluation for black-box models.
Notwithstanding, the method relies heavily on the quality of the prototypes and is, again, blind to
causal relationships between features. Matching a prototype can also lead to less sparse
counterfactuals, as many features may have to change, and the authors report a trade-off between
sparsity and interpretability.

\citet{mothilal2020explaining} propose DiCE, a framework that generates multiple, diverse and actionable
counterfactuals for machine learning classifiers. It moves explainability from showing a single path of
recourse to offering individuals a variety of options to choose from. DiCE initialises $k$ candidates
randomly and optimises them using a determinantal point process (DPP) term to ensure diversity, along
with proximity to the original instance. The novelty of the search mechanism is that it optimises all
candidates jointly. The framework is evaluated on the COMPAS, Adult-Income, German-Credit and Lending
Club datasets. Its limitations are that it assumes a differentiable and static model, and that it varies
features independently, which can produce causally infeasible counterfactuals; causal constraints are
only used to filter these out afterwards.

\subsection{Evolutionary and Surrogate-Based Methods}
\label{subsec:lr-heuristic-search}
\citet{white2019measurable} introduce CLEAR and a definition of the fidelity of local explanation models
to the underlying classifier, based on distances to a decision boundary. The method generates
b-counterfactuals, which state the minimum changes necessary to flip the classifier's prediction, and
uses them to measure and improve the fidelity of local regression models. Across five case studies,
CLEAR improves on the fidelity of LIME by an average of over 40\%. However, its neighbourhood datasets
do not always represent the relevant regions of the feature space, fidelity varies considerably between
datasets, and b-counterfactuals that change several features are left as future work.

\citet{dandl2020multiobjective} propose MOC which, to the best of the authors' knowledge, is the first
method to formalise the counterfactual search as a multi-objective optimisation problem. It adapts the
NSGA-II algorithm to return a set of Pareto-optimal counterfactuals with different trade-offs between
four objectives: reaching the desired prediction, staying close to the original instance, changing few
features and staying close to the observed data. The method is model-agnostic and can be applied to any
black-box model. Nevertheless, the benchmark is limited to binary classification on datasets with at
most 40 features, which raises concerns about scalability to high-dimensional data. The work also leaves
open how one should choose the final counterfactual from the Pareto front, gives no guarantee of causal
consistency, and can be computationally expensive due to the nature of evolutionary algorithms.

\section{Plausibility and Causal Consistency}
\label{sec:lr-plausibility}
A counterfactual that is close to the original instance is not necessarily one that a person can reach.
This section reviews two responses to that problem: keeping counterfactuals on the data manifold, and
explicitly modelling the causal relationships between features.

\subsection{Data-Manifold Methods}
\label{subsec:lr-data-manifold}
\citet{poyiadzi2020face} propose FACE, a graph-based method for generating counterfactuals. It constructs
a graph where nodes are observed data points (e.g., people's financial profiles), edges connect nearby
nodes, and density-weighted edge weights represent the cost of moving from one node to another, which
favours paths through high-density regions. The method then searches for the shortest path from the
original instance to a node of the desired class that satisfies prediction and density thresholds. On a
synthetic dataset and on MNIST, the authors show that the method generates not only counterfactuals but
also intermediate steps that explain how to reach them. However, the method does not guarantee that
real-world actions will succeed: it ensures plausibility, which should not be confused with causality,
nor with proof that an individual can take that set of actions. Evaluation on real-world data of a
dynamic nature is left as future work.

\citet{Olson_2021} introduce counterfactual state explanations for deep reinforcement learning agents via
generative deep learning. An encoder maps the game state to a latent representation, which is fed
together with the agent's action distribution into a generator. The result is the minimal visual
modification that makes the agent choose a different action. The networks are trained using a
combination of adversarial and reconstruction losses, so that the counterfactual states are both
realistic and close to the original state. Two user studies on Atari games with non-experts show that
(i) the generated states have sufficient fidelity to the actual game, and (ii) they help participants
identify a flawed agent, which the authors deliberately blinded to its own ship's position, more
effectively than a nearest-neighbour baseline or no explanation at all. The method could therefore serve
as an auditing tool to identify agent misbehaviour before real-world deployment. Beyond being restricted
to reinforcement learning agents and evaluated on Atari games rather than on high-stakes real-world
problems, the method does not explain the long-term effects of the chosen action on the environment, so
no temporal consistency is guaranteed. The generative model also has no concern for causal consistency,
which increases the risk of providing misleading explanations.

\citet{joshi2019towards} introduce REVISE, a method for plausible algorithmic recourse through generative
models. A variational autoencoder (VAE) encodes a person into a latent space, where gradient descent
pushes the candidate towards the desired label while keeping it close to the original instance. Recourse
is the difference between the original and the generated instance. Identified limitations include that
the suggested changes are not sparse, which may make them difficult for a person to act upon; that the
reported evaluation is mainly qualitative, with quantitative results relegated to the appendix; and that
realism depends on the VAE. Although the authors use a method (CEVAE) that captures the effect of a
treatment on the outcome adjusted for confounders (variables that influence both the treatment and the
outcome), they do not model causal relationships between features, so recourse is still a list of
independent feature changes, and they provide no guarantee that it works. They also show that recourse
can act as a diagnostic, revealing whether a classifier is systematically confounded by specific
attributes.

\subsection{Causal Constraints and Recourse as Interventions}
\label{subsec:lr-causal-recourse}
\citet{mahajan2019preserving} are among the first to connect the feasibility of counterfactuals with
causal relationships between features, arguing that feasibility is fundamentally a causal concept. They
use a VAE-based generator with constraints derived from a partial structural causal model (SCM) and, when
such constraints cannot be expressed, learn feasibility from users' binary feedback. However, causal
relationships are soft constraints and are therefore not guaranteed to be satisfied, the output is still
a counterfactual state rather than a set of actions, and the comparison covers only one external baseline.

\citet{karimi2021algorithmic}, in contrast, present a method that outputs actions as interventions on an
SCM, thus guaranteeing causal consistency when the SCM is correct. They prove that acting on a nearest
counterfactual explanation is only guaranteed to achieve recourse when the acted-upon variables have no
descendants, and that a minimal intervention never costs more than a counterfactual-based action that
achieves recourse. The method extends the aforementioned MACE framework and distinguishes immutable,
mutable but non-actionable, and actionable features, successfully reframing recourse from a nearest-point
search to actions and their consequences. Regarding limitations, the method requires the true SCM, with
no hidden confounders and invertible equations, and the experiments are limited to small graphs, with
little robustness analysis. The authors leave open (i) actions that affect multiple features,
(ii) sequential actions and (iii) the choice of cost functions.

\citet{karimi2020algorithmic} tackle the weakness of having to know the true SCM. They start by showing
that two causal models that are observationally equivalent can lead to different recourse
recommendations, so unless we know the true structural equations, we cannot guarantee that recourse is
valid, no matter how much data is available. They then propose two probabilistic methods for the case
where the structural equations are unknown but the causal graph is known: individual recourse using a
Gaussian process SCM (GP-SCM), and subpopulation recourse based on the conditional average treatment
effect (CATE). The first assumes that each structural equation is a function of its parents plus Gaussian
noise, yielding a distribution over a person's counterfactuals. The second drops all assumptions about
the structural equations and estimates the average effect of the action on people who share the same
non-descendant features. The limitations are that (i) the causal graph is still required, as only the
equations are relaxed, (ii) the additive Gaussian noise assumption may not hold under the true SCM,
leading to sub-optimal solutions, (iii) the experiments are small, and (iv) subpopulation recourse only
ensures that the average effect of the action is positive for a group of people, not that it works for
a given individual. The authors treat the classifier as given, and they point to fair decision making
with a classifier that evolves over time as an important direction for future research.

\section{Fairness of Recourse}
\label{sec:lr-fair-recourse}
Even when recourse is valid, it may be harder for some people to achieve than for others. This section
reviews how that inequality has been measured, first through distance-based audits, then through causal
notions of fairness, and finally over time.

\citet{sharma2020certifai} propose CERTIFAI, a framework that uses counterfactual explanations generated by
a genetic algorithm as a model-agnostic way of auditing machine learning models for fairness, robustness
and interpretability. They introduce CERScore, a black-box robustness metric defined as the average
distance between inputs and their counterfactuals, and burden, the expected distance to the counterfactual
for a group, which allows groups to be compared. The main limitations are that a genetic approach does
not guarantee optimality, that actionability depends on feature constraints set by the user, and that
causal consistency is not considered.

\citet{vonkugelgen2022fairness} make recourse a fairness criterion and connect it formally to counterfactual
fairness. They define group-level and individual-level notions of fair causal recourse and show that:
(i) group-level fairness does not imply individual-level fairness; (ii) a counterfactually fair classifier
does not guarantee fair recourse (fair prediction and fair recourse are complementary, not substitutes);
and (iii) if the classifier only uses features that are non-descendants of the protected attribute, and the
feasible actions and their costs do not change under a counterfactual change of that attribute, then
recourse is individually fair. They also show that non-causal metrics misrepresent recourse unfairness, and
they detect recourse discrimination in a case study on the Adult dataset. Regarding limitations, the
individual-level notion needs a counterfactual on the protected attribute, which requires the full SCM;
the fix based on non-descendant features can lead to a drop in accuracy; constraining training directly is
difficult because the search over intervention targets is combinatorial; and the individual-level measure
is a maximum over individuals, so it may be dominated by outliers. As changing the classifier does not
address the root of the problem, the authors also propose societal interventions on the data-generating
process as an alternative.

\citet{fonseca2023setting} move the question to a dynamic setting. In agent-based simulations, they define a
recourse reliability metric and study two effects: competition with other agents acting on recourse, and
competition with new agents entering the environment. Only a small set of parameter settings yields recourse
that is reliable over time. However, these results rest on simulated adaptation functions, which only
approximate human behaviour, and no causal model is used.

\citet{bell2025much} build on this simulation work and define two new metrics, grounded in substantive equality
of opportunity, for measuring fairness in this context: Effort-to-Recourse (ETR) and Time-to-Recourse (TTR).
Disadvantaged groups need more of both, even under a fair classifier. The proposed Circumstance-Normalized
Selection (CNS) adjusts selection after scoring to reduce that gap. In terms of limitations, the work relies
on agent simulations, so its results depend on assumed human behaviour, it uses no causal models, and the
experiments use a simple logistic classifier. The authors also point to the lack of public real-world
recourse data as a main limitation.

\section{Recourse and Causal Models Under Drift}
\label{sec:lr-drift}
Most of the works above assume that the model, the population and the causal mechanisms stay fixed. This
section relaxes that assumption in three steps: the world changes and invalidates recourse, recourse itself
causes change, and causal structure has to be learned from change.

\subsection{Recourse in a Changing World}
\label{subsec:lr-changing-world}
One of the most relevant works on recourse in changing environments is \citet{de2025time}, where the authors
treat the world as a causal process that changes over time, rather than treating the model as the only
shifting component. They start by proving that counterfactual recourse for a future time cannot be optimal
unless the noise has zero variance, and that robust uncertainty sets are not enough (i.e., searching for a
counterfactual and giving it an error margin does not counteract time). Their solution, T-SAR, gives
subpopulation recourse using an estimator of the stochastic process. Notwithstanding, the estimator is fitted
once and the classifier is also trained once, so trends in the classifier and in the cost are not studied,
and the results depend on the quality of the estimator. The work covers one class of time-series SCM
(TiMINo), does not identify which mechanism changed, and has no fairness analysis.

\citet{kostrzewa2026counterfactual} repair counterfactuals as the classifier updates, instead of making a
prediction about the future, keeping a set of counterfactuals valid and plausible across time. A
counterfactual is repaired when it becomes invalid or its target-class confidence is low, and it is retired
once the updated classifier already gives the original instance the target class. Repairing is cheaper than
regenerating, although repaired counterfactuals can move further from the original instance. The main
limitation of this work is that it is a proof of concept: it uses a local linear surrogate, does not model
causal relationships, has no fairness mechanisms, and covers only gradual drift on low-dimensional synthetic
datasets.

\citet{upadhyay2021towards} propose ROAR, a framework that hardens recourse in advance against a bounded model
shift through a minimax objective, and prove that the extra cost of this robustness is bounded by a 1-Lipschitz
function. Unfortunately, the framework applies only to linear models (non-linear models are first approximated
using LIME) and to non-causal recourse. It also assumes bounded shifts rather than continuous drift.

\citet{rawal2020algorithmic} give this problem a theoretical basis. They establish a lower bound on the
probability that recourse is invalidated by a model update, and show a trade-off between that probability and
recourse cost. Experiments on temporal, geospatial and data-correction shifts confirm that invalidation happens
in practice, and that causally generated recourse is not immune to it. The work offers no solution, however,
and its theory covers only sparse counterfactual recourse.

\subsection{Recourse That Causes Its Own Drift}
\label{subsec:lr-recourse-drift}
\citet{konig2026performative} define performative validity as whether recourse still holds under the best model
after people act on it. Their work proves that recourse can fail when actions intervene on, or are influenced
by, effect variables. Improvement-focused causal recourse stays valid across a broad range of settings, whereas
standard counterfactual and causal recourse often do not. The main limitations are that the work considers a
single step, without repeated applications, that it assumes a single, static SCM, and that it requires causal
knowledge.

\citet{liu2025understanding}, on the other hand, follow the loop over many rounds with a limited number of positive
decisions. Recourse pushes decision standards up, raising cost and failure rates. Fair-top-k and Dynamic Continual
Learning reduce the damage, with recourse costs falling by around 70\%: the former encourages diversity in the
feature space by favouring people from less crowded regions, and the latter reduces how sharply the model changes.
Here, there is no causal model, and the theory focuses on logistic regression in a saturated system. In both
papers, drift comes from recourse and not from the outside world.

\subsection{Learning Causal Structure from Change}
\label{subsec:lr-learning-causal-structure}
\citet{huang2020causal} propose Constraint-based causal Discovery from heterogeneous/NOnstationary Data (CD-NOD), a
framework that leverages distribution shifts to learn causal structure. It also extracts a low-dimensional
``driving force'' of the change and allows edges to appear and vanish over time. \citet{perry2022causal} make
sparsity explicit through the sparse mechanism shift hypothesis and propose the Mechanism Shift Score, proving
that the full graph can be identified with high probability if the hypothesis holds. Although promising, both
works are limited in scale: they are batch methods, so neither fits a data stream directly, and the kernel-based
tests used by CD-NOD have a cost of $O(N^3)$ in the number of samples. Both also rule out most hidden confounders,
sparse shift remains a hypothesis rather than an established fact, and neither work produces counterfactuals or
recourse.

\section{Readable Structural Equations}
\label{sec:lr-readable-structural-equations}
\citet{ma2025causal} propose Causal-KAN, which puts SCM functions into KANs and encodes causal direction through
initialisation and constraints, claiming less overfitting and more readable mappings. Alongside it,
\citet{almodovar2026kolmogorov} show that KANs can be used to learn the structural equations of a known causal
graph, so that the learned mechanisms can be inspected directly. Their method assumes causal sufficiency, and the
authors note that it cannot guarantee that the learned mechanisms coincide with the true structural equations.

Additive structural equations also have a statistical precursor in \citet{buhlmann2014cam}, whose causal additive
models (CAM) separate the search for a variable order from the sparse selection of edges, with consistency results
in both low and high dimensions. CAM assumes that there are no hidden variables or cycles, and the authors note that
such models are not closed under marginalisation, so a hidden variable can take the data outside the model class.

Nevertheless, none of these works provides any connection to recourse, much less to drift and fairness.
