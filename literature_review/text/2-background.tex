This subsection fixes the notation used in the rest of the document. Uppercase italic letters
denote random variables, such as a feature $X_j$, the outcome $Y$ or the protected attribute $A$,
and the corresponding lowercase letters denote the values they take ($x_j$, $y$, $a$). Bold letters
denote vectors or sets of variables: $\mathbf{X} = (X_1, \dots, X_d)$ collects the $d$ features and
$\mathbf{x}$ holds the feature values of one person. Calligraphic letters denote graphs and sets,
such as the causal graph $\mathcal{G}$, the set $\mathcal{I}$ of intervened variables and the set
$\mathcal{F}(\mathbf{x}^{\mathtt{F}})$ of feasible actions of a person; $\mathcal{N}$ and $\mathrm{Unif}$ denote the
normal and uniform distributions. The index $j$ refers to the variable being explained, $i$, $k$
and $l$ to its parents or to intervened variables, $m$ to a basis function and $t$ to a time
window. A hat marks a quantity estimated from data, as in $\hat{f}_j$ for the estimate of $f_j$.
The letter $\sigma$ has two uses, kept apart by their form: $\sigma(z)$, always written with an
argument, is the logistic function, and $\sigma_j^2$, always written with a subscript, is the
noise variance of $X_j$.

\subsection{Structural Causal Models and Interventions}
\label{sec:bg-scm}

Recourse asks what would happen to a person's decision if they acted, and counterfactual fairness
asks what the decision would have been had a protected attribute been different. Both are, inevitably, causal
questions, and this thesis answers them with structural causal models (SCMs)
\cite{pearl2009causality, peters2017elements}. This subsection introduces SCMs and interventions;
counterfactuals computed from an SCM follow in Section~\ref{sec:bg-recourse}, where they are first
needed.

\paragraph{Structural causal models.}
An SCM describes how each variable is produced from its direct causes. Following
\citet{peters2017elements}, an SCM over $X_1, \dots, X_d$ consists of $d$ structural
assignments
\begin{equation}
  X_j := f_j(\mathbf{PA}_j, U_j), \qquad j = 1, \dots, d,
  \label{eq:bg-scm}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{PA}_j \subseteq \{X_1, \dots, X_d\} \setminus \{X_j\}$ are called the parents of
$X_j$, together with a joint distribution $P_{\mathbf{U}}$ over the noise variables
$U_1, \dots, U_d$, which are required to be jointly independent. \citet{peters2017elements} write
the noise variables as $N_j$. This thesis writes $U_j$, the letter used by \citet{pearl2009overview}
and the recourse literature \cite{karimi2021algorithmic}, where $U_j$ is called an exogenous
variable and $X_j$ an endogenous one. Drawing an arrow from each parent to its child gives the
causal graph $\mathcal{G}$, which \citet{peters2017elements} assume to be acyclic. In Pearl's
formulation and in the recourse literature, independent noise is not part of the definition but
an extra assumption, which this thesis makes: \citet{pearl2009overview} calls a model Markovian
when its graph is acyclic and its exogenous variables are jointly independent.

The parents of $X_j$ are its direct causes, and $U_j$ gathers everything else that affects $X_j$
but is not modelled. Requiring the noise variables to be independent therefore rules out hidden
confounders, that is, unobserved variables that affect two or more of the modelled ones; this
assumption is called causal sufficiency. An acyclic SCM entails a single joint distribution over
$X_1, \dots, X_d$ \cite{peters2017elements}, and in a Markovian model this distribution
factorises into one conditional distribution per variable
\cite{pearl2009causality, pearl2009overview}:
\begin{equation}
  P(X_1, \dots, X_d) = \prod_{j=1}^{d} P(X_j \mid \mathbf{PA}_j).
  \label{eq:bg-markov}
\end{equation}
Each factor is the causal mechanism of one variable. The principle of independent
mechanisms states that these factors do not inform or influence one another, so that one of them
can change while the others stay the same \cite{peters2017elements}. Without causal sufficiency,
Equation~\ref{eq:bg-markov} no longer holds in general. One solution is to add the hidden
confounder to the model as a latent variable. \citet{louizos2017causal}, for example, infer an
unobserved confounder $Z$ from observed proxies of it, assuming that the proxies carry enough
information to recover the joint distribution of $Z$ and the observed variables; since $Z$ is
the only hidden confounder in their model, adding it makes the model Markovian again.


\paragraph{Running example.}
Figure~\ref{fig:bg-running-example} shows the SCM used as a running example. A person's income
$X_1$ affects their savings $X_2$, and both affect the bank's decision $Y$, where $Y = 1$ means
that the loan is approved:
\begin{equation}
  \begin{array}{rcl}
    X_1 & := & U_1, \\[2pt]
    X_2 & := & 0.5\, X_1^2 + U_2, \\[2pt]
    Y   & := & \mathbf{1}\!\left[\, U_Y < \sigma(-4 + X_1 + 0.5\, X_2) \,\right],
  \end{array}
  \label{eq:bg-example}
\end{equation}
with $U_1 \sim \mathrm{Unif}(0, 4)$, $U_2 \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 0.3^2)$ and
$U_Y \sim \mathrm{Unif}(0, 1)$, where $\sigma(z) = 1 / (1 + e^{-z})$ is the logistic function and
$\mathbf{1}[\cdot]$ equals 1 when its condition holds and 0 otherwise. The last equation means
that a person is approved with probability $\sigma(-4 + X_1 + 0.5\, X_2)$. The noise $U_2$ can be
read as a saving habit: a positive value means that the person saves more than others with the
same income.

\paragraph{Interventions.}
An intervention sets a variable from outside the system. The intervention
$\mathrm{do}(X_i = \theta)$ replaces the equation of $X_i$ with the constant $\theta$ and leaves
every other equation unchanged \cite{pearl2009causality, pearl2009overview}:
\begin{equation}
  X_i := \theta, \qquad X_j := f_j(\mathbf{PA}_j, U_j) \quad \mbox{for all } j \neq i.
  \label{eq:bg-do}
\end{equation}
The result is a new SCM, whose graph has no arrows into $X_i$
(Figure~\ref{fig:interventions}) and whose distribution is the
\emph{interventional} distribution $P(X_1, \dots, X_d \mid \mathrm{do}(X_i = \theta))$. Several
variables can be set at once. Following \citet{karimi2020algorithmic}, this thesis writes
$\mathrm{do}(\mathbf{X}_{\mathcal{I}} = \boldsymbol{\theta})$ for the intervention that sets every
$X_i$ with $i$ in a set of indices $\mathcal{I} \subseteq \{1, \dots, d\}$ to the matching value
$\theta_i$; \citet{pearl2009overview} writes $\mathrm{do}(X = x)$, with $X$ itself a set of
variables. Intervening is not the
same as conditioning. In the running example, $P(Y \mid X_2 = x_2)$ describes the people who
happen to have savings $x_2$, and those with high savings tend to also have a high income,
whereas $P(Y \mid \mathrm{do}(X_2 = x_2))$ describes what happens when savings are set to $x_2$
and income stays as it was. In algorithmic recourse, the action a person takes is modelled as an intervention
on the features they can change \cite{karimi2021algorithmic}.

This subsection introduces counterfactual explanations and algorithmic recourse: what they are,
what they are used for and why they matter.

% Sources: Karimi et al. (2022), arXiv:2010.04050, Sec. 2.1 (definitions, Ustun et al. among them,
% and the unified "understand and act" definition), Sec. 2.2 (Q1/Q2, consequential

\paragraph{Recourse.}
Algorithmic recourse has been defined in several overlapping ways. We define it by merging the definitions of
\citet{ustun2019actionable} and 
\citet{karimi2022survey}: a person who receives an unfavourable
decision achieves recourse when they can understand a model's decision and act to reverse it by altering
actionable input variables. A rejected
loan applicant, such as a person in the running example of Section~\ref{sec:bg-scm}, therefore
needs answers to two questions~\cite{karimi2022survey}: which profile would have led to the loan being approved (Q1), and
which actions would have led to that profile (Q2). An answer to Q1 is a counterfactual explanation
and an answer to Q2 is a recommendation of actions. Consider the statement:
\begin{quote}
  \emph{If I had dropped this glass, it would have broken.}
\end{quote}
It describes what would have followed had something been different. A statement of this form is a
counterfactual conditional, or counterfactual for short. The
antecedent, \emph{I dropped this glass}, is false, and the statement says what would have followed
had it been true. Writing $D$ for the antecedent and $B$ for the consequent, \emph{the glass broke},
\citet{lewis1973counterfactuals} denotes the counterfactual by
\begin{equation}
  D \mathrel{\Box\!\!\!\rightarrow} B,
  \label{eq:bg-lewis}
\end{equation}  
which we read as ``if $D$ were the case, $B$ would be the case''. \citet{lewis1973counterfactuals}
interprets this counterfactual in terms of possible worlds, ordered according 
to their similarity to the actual world. To evaluate Equation~\ref{eq:bg-lewis}, 
one compares worlds in which the glass is dropped and breaks with those in which 
it is dropped but does not break. More precisely, according to \citet[Analysis~3]{lewis1973counterfactuals},
the counterfactual is true if some possible world in which the glass is dropped and breaks 
is more similar to the actual world than every possible world in which it is dropped but 
does not break. If no possible world makes $D$ true, the counterfactual is vacuously true.

\paragraph{Counterfactual explanations.}
\citet{wachter2017counterfactual} bring Lewis's ideas to machine learning. They define a
counterfactual explanation as a statement that gives the feature values behind a decision and the
values that would have led to a different decision, with all other features held constant. Many
such statements exist for the same decision, and the aim is to find the one that 
changes the features as little as
possible, and so describes the ``closest possible world'' in which the decision changes, a notion
they take from \citet{lewis1973counterfactuals}. Similarity between worlds thus becomes a distance
between feature vectors. Wachter et al.\ propose the Manhattan ($L_1$) distance with each feature
weighted by the inverse of its median absolute deviation (MAD), which favours explanations that change
only a few features, and they compute the explanation by minimising this distance together with
the squared gap between the model's output and the desired output. They also point out that their
approach does not rely on knowledge of the causal structure of the world.

\citet{karimi2022survey} write the same problem as a constrained optimisation. Let $h$ be a binary
classifier, $\mathcal{X}$ the space of feature vectors and $\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X}) \subseteq
\mathcal{X}$ the plausible ones, those consistent with the distribution of training data, and let
$\mathbf{x}^{\mathtt{F}}$ be the observed (factual) features of a person. A nearest counterfactual
explanation is then
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{x}^{*\mathtt{CFE}} \in \mathop{\mathrm{arg\,min}}_{\mathbf{x}^{\mathtt{CFE}} \in
  \mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})} \mathrm{dist}(\mathbf{x}^{\mathtt{CFE}}, \mathbf{x}^{\mathtt{F}})
  \quad \mbox{subject to} \quad
  \begin{array}[t]{l}
    h(\mathbf{x}^{\mathtt{CFE}}) \neq h(\mathbf{x}^{\mathtt{F}}), \\
    \mathbf{x}^{\mathtt{CFE}} = \mathbf{x}^{\mathtt{F}} + \boldsymbol{\delta},
  \end{array}  
  \label{eq:bg-cfe}
\end{equation}  
where $\boldsymbol{\delta}$ shifts each feature independently of the others. Karimi et al.\
describe these solutions as feature combinations in nearby worlds, again citing
\citet{lewis1973counterfactuals}. A solution of Equation~\ref{eq:bg-cfe} answers Q1. It does not answer Q2: 
although a counterfactual explanation yields a feature vector that would have achieved recourse, 
the actions that would lead the person to it cannot in general be 
inferred from it \cite{karimi2021algorithmic}.

\paragraph{Structural counterfactuals.}
An SCM gives a concrete answer to which world is closest to the actual one.
\citet[Sec.~3.4]{pearl2009overview} reads ``had $X$ been $x$'' as a minimal change to the model:
the counterfactual world keeps every other structural assignment and the person's own noise values,
and changes only what the intervention sets.~\citet[Sec.~7.4.1]{pearl2009causality} relates this
reading to Lewis's counterfactuals. Given the factual features $\mathbf{x}^{\mathtt{F}}$ of a person
and an intervention $\mathrm{do}(\mathbf{X}_{\mathcal{I}} = \boldsymbol{\theta})$, the
counterfactual is computed in three steps \citep[Thm.~7.1.7]{pearl2009causality}, which
\citet{vonkugelgen2022fairness} also follow for recourse. In the abduction step, the distribution
of the noise is updated with the observation $\mathbf{x}^{\mathtt{F}}$. In the action step, the
assignments of the variables in $\mathcal{I}$ are replaced as in Equation~\ref{eq:bg-do}. In the
prediction step, the modified assignments are evaluated under the updated noise distribution, which
gives a distribution over the counterfactual features \citep[Sec.~7.1.3]{pearl2009causality}. When
only one value of the noise is consistent with $\mathbf{x}^{\mathtt{F}}$, abduction returns that
value, $\mathbf{u}^{\mathtt{F}}$, and the counterfactual is a single feature vector. Following \citet{vonkugelgen2022fairness}, it is written
$\mathbf{x}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathcal{I}}}(\mathbf{u}^{\mathtt{F}})$: the features the person
would have had, had $\mathbf{X}_{\mathcal{I}}$ been set to $\boldsymbol{\theta}$.
In the running example the noise is additive and can be read off the
features: $u_1 = x_1$ and $u_2 = x_2 - 0.5\,x_1^2$. A person with $\mathbf{x}^{\mathtt{F}} = (2, 2)$
thus has $u_2 = 0$, and under $\mathrm{do}(X_1 = 3)$ their savings become $0.5 \cdot 3^2 + 0 = 4.5$,
so that, with $\mathcal{I} = \{1\}$ and $\boldsymbol{\theta} = 3$,
$\mathbf{x}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathcal{I}}}(\mathbf{u}^{\mathtt{F}}) = (3, 4.5)$. 

\paragraph{Consequential recommendations.}
\citet{karimi2021algorithmic} model an action as an intervention in an SCM of the world in which
the person acts, so that its effects on the other variables are taken into account. A recommended
set of such actions is called a consequential recommendation \cite{karimi2022survey}. Following
\citet{vonkugelgen2022fairness}, the action $\mathrm{do}(\mathbf{X}_{\mathcal{I}} =
\boldsymbol{\theta})$ is written $\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathcal{I}}$ for short, and
$\mathcal{F}(\mathbf{x}^{\mathtt{F}})$ denotes the set of feasible actions, those that the person can
perform. The actions that change the decision are then
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{R} = \left\{ \boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathcal{I}} \in \mathcal{F}(\mathbf{x}^{\mathtt{F}})
  \mid h(\mathbf{x}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathcal{I}}}(\mathbf{u}^{\mathtt{F}})) \neq
  h(\mathbf{x}^{\mathtt{F}}) \right\},
  \label{eq:bg-recourse}
\end{equation}
and, given the cost $c(\mathbf{x}^{\mathtt{F}}, \boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathcal{I}})$ of an action for
the person, a minimal consequential recommendation is the cheapest of them \cite{karimi2022survey}:
\begin{equation}
  \boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}_{\mathcal{I}^{*}} \in \mathop{\mathrm{arg\,min}}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathcal{I}}
  \in \mathcal{F}(\mathbf{x}^{\mathtt{F}})} c(\mathbf{x}^{\mathtt{F}}, \boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathcal{I}})
  \quad \mbox{subject to} \quad
  h(\mathbf{x}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathcal{I}}}(\mathbf{u}^{\mathtt{F}})) \neq h(\mathbf{x}^{\mathtt{F}}).
  \label{eq:bg-min-recourse}
\end{equation}
Because notation across papers differ, we will now attempt to clarify it. \citet{karimi2022survey} write the action as
$a$, the set of feasible actions as $\mathcal{A}(\mathbf{x}^{\mathtt{F}})$, the counterfactual as
$\mathbf{x}^{\mathtt{SCF}}(a; \mathbf{x}^{\mathtt{F}})$ and the cost as
$\mathrm{cost}(a; \mathbf{x}^{\mathtt{F}})$, whereas \citet{vonkugelgen2022fairness} write the set
of feasible actions as $\mathcal{F}(\mathbf{x}^{\mathtt{F}})$. This thesis follows the latter,
because $a$ denotes a value of the protected attribute $A$, and $\mathcal{A}$ could be read as the
set of protected attributes. Unlike
Equation~\ref{eq:bg-cfe}, Equation~\ref{eq:bg-min-recourse} requires an SCM. Its solution also
gives a counterfactual explanation, $\mathbf{x}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}_{\mathcal{I}^{*}}}
(\mathbf{u}^{\mathtt{F}})$, though not necessarily the nearest one, whereas a solution of
Equation~\ref{eq:bg-cfe} gives no action. For this reason, \citet{karimi2022survey} argue that
recourse should primarily be offered through minimal consequential recommendations, and that
nearest counterfactual explanations are of secondary importance.

The running example shows the difference. Suppose that the bank approves a loan when the
probability of approval is at least $0.5$, that is, $h(\mathbf{x}) = \mathbf{1}[x_1 + 0.5\,x_2
\geq 4]$, and take again the person with $\mathbf{x}^{\mathtt{F}} = (2, 2)$, who is rejected
because $2 + 0.5 \cdot 2 = 3 < 4$. With the unweighted $L_1$ distance and no plausibility
constraint, the nearest counterfactual explanation raises income by one unit,
$\mathbf{x}^{*\mathtt{CFE}} = (3, 2)$. However, raising income to 3 does not lead to this profile.
As computed above, savings also rise, to 4.5, so the person ends up at $(3, 4.5)$. The explanation
describes a profile that the person cannot reach by changing income alone, and it asks for a larger
change than needed. Suppose that the person can change income, savings or both, and that the cost
of an action is the total amount by which it changes them. The minimal consequential recommendation
then changes income only. If income is set to $\theta$, savings become $0.5\,\theta^2$, and the
loan is approved once $\theta + 0.25\,\theta^2 \geq 4$, that is, once $\theta$ reaches about
$2.47$. Raising income by about $0.47$ is therefore enough, because savings rise to about $3.06$
along with it, whereas the explanation asked for a rise of $1$.

Machine learning models increasingly inform decisions in high-stakes domains, such as
healthcare~\cite{shailaja2018machine} and hiring~\cite{raghavan2020mitigating}, where their
predictions directly affect people's lives. In such domains, predicting well is not enough: the
decisions must also be fair to the people they affect. \citet{mitchell2021algorithmic} note that a
model is now commonly called biased when its predictive performance unjustifiably differs across
disadvantaged groups, defined along social axes such as race, gender and class, and describe
uncovering and rectifying such biases as the motivation of the field of fairness in machine
learning. This subsection presents the two families of fairness criteria that the rest of the
thesis relies on: statistical criteria, which compare groups of people, and causal criteria, which
ask what the decision would have been had the protected attribute been different.

\paragraph{Statistical criteria.}
In this subsection the classifier may use the protected attribute $A$, such as sex or race, so it
is applied to all of a person's variables, $\mathbf{v} = (\mathbf{x}, a)$; a classifier that does
not use $A$, as in Section~\ref{sec:bg-recourse}, simply ignores the last entry. Its decision is
written $\hat{Y} = h(\mathbf{V})$, with $\mathbf{V} = (\mathbf{X}, A)$.
\citet[Ch.~3]{barocas2023fairness} group the statistical criteria into three families, called
independence, separation and sufficiency. Each is a conditional independence between $A$, the
outcome $Y$ and the decision; in this order, they require
\begin{equation}
  \hat{Y} \perp A, \qquad
  \hat{Y} \perp A \mid Y, \qquad
  Y \perp A \mid \hat{Y}.
  \label{eq:bg-group-criteria}
\end{equation}
For a binary decision, independence requires the approval rate $P(\hat{Y} = 1 \mid A = a)$ to be
the same for every value $a$ of $A$; it is also called demographic parity or statistical parity.
Separation requires every group to have the same true positive rate
$P(\hat{Y} = 1 \mid Y = 1, A = a)$ and the same false positive rate
$P(\hat{Y} = 1 \mid Y = 0, A = a)$. \citet{hardt2016equality} call it equalized odds, and call the
weaker requirement of equal true positive rates alone equal opportunity. Unlike demographic parity,
separation lets the decision depend on $A$, but only through the outcome $Y$
\cite{hardt2016equality}. Sufficiency requires that people who receive the same decision have the
same probability of a positive outcome in every group, that is, equal positive and negative
predictive values. The three criteria conflict with one another: for a binary outcome whose rate
differs between groups, so that $A$ and $Y$ are dependent, no two of them can hold at the same time
except in degenerate cases \citep[Ch.~3]{barocas2023fairness}.

\paragraph{Counterfactual fairness.}
The criteria above depend only on the joint distribution of $A$, $\mathbf{X}$, $Y$ and the
decision, which is why they are called observational \cite{kilbertus2017avoiding}. As a result,
they cannot say what caused a disparity \citep[Ch.~3]{barocas2023fairness}.
\citet{kilbertus2017avoiding} recall two scenarios, constructed by \citet{hardt2016equality}, that
have intuitively different social interpretations but the same joint distribution, so that no
observational criterion can tell them apart. Causal criteria instead reason about an SCM in which
$A$ is one of the variables. \citet{kusner2017counterfactual} call a decision counterfactually fair
if, for every person, it would have been the same in a counterfactual world in which the person
belonged to a different demographic group. Formally, given the observed values $\mathbf{x}$ and
$a$, the decision must have the same distribution under $\mathrm{do}(A = a')$ as under
$\mathrm{do}(A = a)$, for every value $a'$ of $A$. The counterfactuals are computed with the three
steps of Section~\ref{sec:bg-recourse}, now intervening on $A$. When only one value of the noise,
$\mathbf{u}^{\mathtt{F}}$, is consistent with the observed variables
$\mathbf{v}^{\mathtt{F}} = (\mathbf{x}^{\mathtt{F}}, a^{\mathtt{F}})$, the counterfactual is a
single vector $\mathbf{v}_a(\mathbf{u}^{\mathtt{F}})$, called the person's counterfactual twin had
$A$ been $a$, and the criterion reads \cite{vonkugelgen2022fairness}
\begin{equation}
  h(\mathbf{v}^{\mathtt{F}}) = h(\mathbf{v}_a(\mathbf{u}^{\mathtt{F}}))
  \quad \mbox{for every value } a \mbox{ of } A \mbox{ and every person.}
  \label{eq:bg-cf-fairness}
\end{equation}
Leaving $A$ out of the classifier's inputs, which \citet{kusner2017counterfactual} call fairness
through unawareness, does not ensure this, because other features can carry information about
$A$: in the twin, every feature that $A$ affects changes as well. A classifier that uses only
variables that are not descendants of $A$ in the causal graph, on the other hand, is always
counterfactually fair \citep[Lemma~1]{kusner2017counterfactual}.

\paragraph{Path-specific fairness.}
Counterfactual fairness treats every effect of $A$ on the decision as unfair
\cite{chiappa2019path}, and the most direct way to satisfy it is to discard every descendant of
$A$ \cite{kusner2017counterfactual}. Some of these effects may be acceptable, however. In the
Berkeley admissions case, discussed by both \citet{kilbertus2017avoiding} and
\citet{chiappa2019path}, women had a lower admission rate than men overall because they applied
more often to competitive departments, and the effect of gender on admission through the choice of
department is not regarded as unfair. \citet{kilbertus2017avoiding} call a variable through which
$A$ influences the decision in such an accepted way a resolving variable, and say that a variable
that is not itself resolving exhibits unresolved discrimination when a directed path from $A$ to it
is not blocked by a resolving variable. \citet{nabi2018fair} formalise discrimination as the effect
of $A$ on the outcome along chosen causal paths, called a path-specific effect, and stress that
which paths are discriminatory depends on the domain: a physical fitness test may be appropriate
for a construction job but not for an accounting one. They then fit the model to a distribution
that is as close as possible to the data distribution while keeping the path-specific effect
within bounds that state how much discrimination is tolerated. \citet{chiappa2019path} combines
this idea with counterfactual fairness: a decision is path-specific counterfactually fair if it
coincides with the one that would have been taken in a counterfactual world in which $A$ had been
different along the unfair paths only.

All of these criteria concern the decision itself. Section~\ref{sec:lr-fair-recourse} reviews how
they have been carried over to recourse, where the quantity compared between groups, or between a
person and their counterfactual twin, is the cost of the action needed to change the decision
\cite{vonkugelgen2022fairness}.


\subsection{Concept Drift and Distribution Shift}
\label{sec:bg-drift}

% Sources: Gama et al. (2014), abstract (drift as a change in the relation between inputs and
% target). Webb et al. (2016), arXiv:1511.03816v6: Sec. 3, Eqs. (1)-(4) (concept = P(X, Y),
% following Gama et al.; drift as a change in it; counterpart of dataset shift), Sec. 4.2.1-4.2.2
% (class and covariate drift, their examples, and Moreno-Torres et al.'s definitions with their
% causal-direction constraints, which Webb et al. question). Moreno-Torres et al. (2012) were
% checked only through Webb et al. Sec. 4.2 and Lu et al. Sec. 2.1, not in the original.
% Lu et al. (2019), arXiv:2004.05785: Sec. 1 (detection, understanding, adaptation), Sec. 2.1
% (definition, Sources I-III, virtual and actual drift), Sec. 2.2 and Fig. 4 (four drift types),
% Sec. 3 (when, how, where). Scholkopf et al. (2021), Sec. 4: ICM principle and the Sparse
% Mechanism Shift hypothesis. Deho et al. (2024), abstract.
A model is trained on data from the past and applied to people in the present. When the data change
in between, a model that was accurate when it was trained can become outdated.
\citet{gama2014survey} describe this problem, concept drift, as a change over time in the relation
between the input data and the target variable. Following them, \citet{webb2016characterizing} call
the joint distribution $P_t(\mathbf{X}, Y)$ of the features and the outcome in time window $t$ the
concept at time $t$, and most of the literature now says that concept drift occurs when this
distribution changes \cite{lu2019learning}:
\begin{equation}
  P_t(\mathbf{X}, Y) \neq P_{t+1}(\mathbf{X}, Y).
  \label{eq:bg-drift}
\end{equation}
\citet{webb2016characterizing} describe concept drift as the stream-learning counterpart of dataset
shift, the name used in batch learning when a model is trained on one dataset and tested on another
\cite{moreno2012unifying}.

\paragraph{What changes.}
Writing $P_t(\mathbf{X}, Y) = P_t(\mathbf{X})\,P_t(Y \mid \mathbf{X})$ separates two sources of
drift \cite{lu2019learning}. When only $P_t(\mathbf{X})$ changes, the population changes but the
relation between features and outcome does not, as when the demographics of a company's customers
change over time \cite{webb2016characterizing}. This does not move the decision boundary, and is
also called virtual drift. When $P_t(Y \mid \mathbf{X})$ changes, the relation itself changes, as
when a new tax law changes which tax-payers count as compliant \cite{webb2016characterizing}. The
decision boundary then moves and the model's accuracy drops, which is why this is called actual
drift. In practice, the two often occur together \cite{lu2019learning}. For dataset shift,
\citet{moreno2012unifying} distinguish covariate shift \cite{SHIMODAIRA2000227}, in which
$P(\mathbf{X})$ changes while $P(Y \mid \mathbf{X})$ stays the same, prior probability shift, in
which $P(Y)$ changes while $P(\mathbf{X} \mid Y)$ stays the same, and concept shift, in which the
relation between features and outcome changes. They also tie covariate shift to problems in which
the features cause the outcome, and prior probability shift to problems in which the outcome causes
the features. \citet{webb2016characterizing} question this restriction, because in many real
problems some features cause the outcome, others are caused by it, and others share causes with it.
The names also vary: \citet{webb2016characterizing} call a change in $P(Y \mid \mathbf{X})$ class
drift, and note that it is also called real concept drift or prior probability shift.

\paragraph{How drift unfolds.}
Drift also differs in how it unfolds over time. \citet{lu2019learning} distinguish four types: in
sudden drift, a new concept appears within a short time; in gradual drift, a new concept gradually
replaces the old one; in incremental drift, the old concept changes step by step into the new one;
and with reoccurring concepts, an old concept returns after some time. They also organise learning
under drift into three tasks: detecting whether drift has occurred, understanding it, and adapting
the model to it. Understanding drift means finding when it occurs and how long it lasts, how severe
it is, and where it occurs, that is, which regions of the data it affects. Methods for detecting
and explaining drift are reviewed in Section~\ref{sec:lr-drift-explanation}.

\paragraph{Drift in a causal model.}
In an SCM, the joint distribution factorises into one mechanism per variable
(Equation~\ref{eq:bg-markov}), and by the principle of independent mechanisms one of them can
change while the others stay the same (Section~\ref{sec:bg-scm}). \citet{scholkopf2021toward} go
further with the sparse mechanism shift hypothesis: small changes in the distribution tend to affect
only a few mechanisms, rather than all of them at once. Drift can then be described by which
mechanisms changed. This turns the question of where drift occurs, which \citet{lu2019learning}
answer with regions of the data, into a question about the causal model, and the categories above
do not answer it: a change in $P(\mathbf{X})$ can come from any of the mechanisms of the features.

The running example shows why this matters for recourse. Suppose that people start saving less, so
that the equation of savings becomes $X_2 := 0.4\,X_1^2 + U_2$, while the equations of income and of
the decision stay the same. Since the equation of $Y$ is unchanged, so is
$P(Y \mid X_1, X_2)$: for the classifier this is covariate shift, and $h$ needs no update. The
consequential recommendation of Section~\ref{sec:bg-recourse}, however, no longer works. For the
person with $\mathbf{x}^{\mathtt{F}} = (2, 2)$, abduction now gives $u_2 = 2 - 0.4 \cdot 4 = 0.4$.
Raising income to $2.47$ now raises savings only to about $2.84$, so the score
$2.47 + 0.5 \cdot 2.84 \approx 3.89$ stays below 4 and the loan is still rejected; under the new
equation, income has to reach about $2.52$. Recourse computed with an outdated causal model can thus
fail even when the classifier itself is still valid. Recourse under drift, and methods that learn
causal structure from changes in the distribution, are reviewed in Section~\ref{sec:lr-drift}.

Drift can also undo fairness. \citet{deho2024still} find that covariate drift is common in real
data and can substantially reduce the fairness of models trained to be fair, and that the size and
direction of the drift do not reliably predict how much fairness is lost. Fairness under
distribution shift is reviewed in Section~\ref{sec:lr-fairness-shift}.
